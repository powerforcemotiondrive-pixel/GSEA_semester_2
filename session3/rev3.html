Session 3: Debate and Critical Thinking

Review the 18 key terms for Debate and Critical Thinking to build your fluency in discussions, professional reading, and TOEIC practice.

Confirmation Bias

01

The psychological tendency to search for, interpret, and favor information that confirms preexisting beliefs while ignoring or dismissing contradictory evidence.

Constructive Disagreement

02

A debate communication method where opposing speakers challenge ideas respectfully using evidence and logic to reach better shared solutions without personal conflict.

Liability Allocation

03

The legal decision of who is responsible (operators, manufacturers, or programmers) when an automated system fails.

Machine Ethics

04

Teaching moral rules and ethical boundaries to automated computer systems and algorithms.

Workforce Disruption

05

Changes and job losses caused when automated machines or AI tools replace human workers.

Cognitive Complacency

06

When people rely too much on automated tools and stop paying close attention to what is happening.

Situational Awareness

07

A pilot's real-time perception and understanding of aircraft status, flight envelope, and atmospheric surroundings.

Algorithmic Accountability

08

The principle that developers, corporations, and governments must be held legally and morally responsible for the outcomes and decisions made by automated systems.

Ethical Dilemma

09

A complex situation in which a decision-maker must choose between two conflicting moral options, neither of which is entirely satisfactory or free from negative consequences.

Counter-Claim / Rebuttal

10

An argument, empirical piece of evidence, or logical reasoning presented directly to challenge, weaken, or disprove an opposing speaker's position in a debate.

Automation Bias

11

Trusting a computer or automated suggestion even when your own judgment warns you something is wrong.

Discourse Concession

12

Acknowledging that an opponent has a valid point before presenting your own counter-argument in a debate.

Social Equity in Automation

13

Ensuring that efficiency gains from automation are shared equitably rather than concentrating profits while eliminating jobs.

Human Factors Engineering

14

Designing technology interfaces tailored to human psychological capabilities and sensory limitations.

Critical Evaluation

15

The systematic examination of arguments, evidence, and underlying assumptions to assess their factual accuracy, logical validity, and fairness before drawing conclusions.

Nuanced Argument

16

A balanced line of reasoning that recognizes complexities, qualifications, and trade-offs rather than presenting an oversimplified black-and-white perspective.

Regulatory Oversight

17

Governmental safety supervision verifying that automated innovations meet rigorous legal and moral benchmarks.

Stakeholder Advocacy

18

Public communication, campaigning, and representation by affected community, worker, or consumer groups to ensure their rights and safety are protected in tech transitions.
